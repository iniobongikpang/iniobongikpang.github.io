Digital Learning - The Ideas Cube Box
Kuchingoro IDP Camp, Games Village - Abuja, Nigeria

To lead step-down trainings and facilitation of Digital Learnings using Information Technology Devices, I led multiple sessions for staff, volunteers and practical sessions for the displaced population in an IDP Camp, Kuchigoro - Abuja.

The Ideas Cube is a compact, 100% autonomous nano-server developed by BSF. It creates a local Wi-Fi hotspot — no internet connection or electricity required — allowing up to 20 users to simultaneously connect via smartphone, tablet, or computer and access preloaded educational and cultural content. Built from 100% recycled plastic with easily replaceable parts, it offers up to 6 hours of battery life and storage of up to 1TB.

OLIP is the digital library platform hosted on the Ideas Cube. It organises content into themes (like library corridors), playlists (like shelves), and applications — covering topics such as health, mental wellbeing, literacy, arts, GBV awareness, vocational skills, and more. Users can browse, watch, read, and download content in visitor mode without needing a password, while Administrators and Facilitators can customise the homepage, manage users, and monitor activity.

Kajou Cards are supplementary content cards that can be installed on the Ideas Cube either online or offline, expanding the library's resources without requiring a permanent internet connection. Sessions included capacity-building session on Conflict-Related Sexual Violence (CRSV) and training on the use of Kajou Cards, an e-library resource that supports facilitators in delivering awareness and learning sessions.

Conflict-Related Sexual Violence (CRSV) is a serious protection concern affecting displaced populations such as internally displaced persons (IDPs), refugees, and asylum seekers. It includes acts like rape, sexual exploitation, forced marriage, and other forms of abuse linked to conflict and insecurity. Displacement often increases vulnerability due to family separation, weak protection systems, unsafe living conditions in camps or informal settlements, and reliance on humanitarian assistance for survival. Reporting of incidences (Survivor or third party) were encouraged through a secured platform for necessary actions.

Overall, the guide equips facilitators with the skills to conduct structured, community-centred sessions using the Ideas Cube — from preparing activities and managing group dynamics to programming content that meets community needs. Participants will understand content creation, video creations, life skills through pre-recorded lessons using android devices and other IT equipment.

DG-ECHO Phase II - Project Performance Review Meeting with Project Staff
Maiduguri, Borno State, Nigeria

A monitoring mission to Maiduguri was conducted from 10–12 December 2025 as part of the quarterly review of the DG-ECHO funded project implemented in Monguno, Dikwa and Ngala LGAs. The visit aimed to assess project implementation progress, review the interim report and supporting documentation, and provide technical feedback to strengthen project quality, accountability, and compliance with donor requirements.

During the quarterly review meeting, I facilitated session with the project staff, where progress against project indicators, operational challenges, and documentation practices were discussed. To ensure Program/Data Quality Assessment (PQA/DQA), the Country Team also carried out a detailed review of project reports and source documents, identifying achievements, gaps, and opportunities for improvement. A Field Learning Review session was facilitated to capture lessons learned and support adaptive programming for the remaining implementation period.

In addition, the project workplan was reviewed and updated to ensure alignment with timelines, activities, and donor expectations. I also faciltated a capacity-building session on Conflict-Related Sexual Violence (CRSV) and training on the use of Kajou Cards, an e-library resource that supports facilitators in delivering awareness and learning sessions.

Overall, the mission strengthened coordination among field teams, improved reporting and documentation practices, and reinforced accountability in project implementation, positioning the project for more effective delivery in the next phase.

October 2025

Post Distribution Monitoring (PDM) Exercise
Mokwa, Niger State, Nigeria

Post Distribution Monitoring (PDM) is an essential step in humanitarian assistance that takes place after aid such as cash, food, or relief items has been delivered to affected populations. It helps organisations understand whether assistance reached the intended beneficiaries, met their priority needs, and was provided in a safe, fair, and dignified manner.

About 2 months after the distribution, I facilitated a session of training and capacity building for community-based enumerators to conduct CAPI/assessment using for the beneficiairy households. PDM strengthens accountability to communities by creating opportunities for beneficiaries to share feedback on the relevance and quality of support received. It also helps identify protection concerns, gaps in targeting, and any unintended negative effects of assistance. This ensures that programmes remain responsive to the realities faced by vulnerable populations, including displaced communities.

The findings from the Post Distribution Monitoring were used to improve programme design and implementation. We were able to adjust distribution methods, enhance communication with communities, strengthen complaint and feedback mechanisms, and ensured that future interventions were more efficient and impactful.

Beyond verification, the PDM provided a continuous learning opportunity. Lessons gathered helped us as humanitarian actors to better understand community preferences, market conditions, and operational challenges. This enables the delivery of more timely, appropriate, and dignified assistance while strengthening transparency and trust with both affected populations and donors.

October 2025

Retrospective Baseline and Midline Assessment for YAPPI
Youth in Animation and Post Production Initiative

Nigeria’s creative industry holds immense potential to drive economic growth and youth empowerment. It has emerged as a critical driver of inclusive growth, innovation, and youth empowerment. However, despite its potential to diversify the national economy and reduce unemployment, the sector continues to face systemic challenges that limit its competitiveness and inclusivity, particularly in urban centers such as Lagos, Enugu, and the Federal Capital Territory (FCT).

Nigeria’s youth unemployment rate remains among the highest globally, estimated at approximately 53%, affecting more than 80 million young people aged 15–35. This economic fragility constrains domestic demand for creative services and limits the ability of young creatives to sustain careers. Moreover, the creative sector’s labor market is characterized by informality and underemployment, with limited social protection for workers.

The Youth in Animation and Post-Production Initiative (YAPPI) was designed to address these gaps by targeting and training youth (aged 18–35, 75% female, 5% PWD) across 3 States of Lagos, Enugu, and FCT, Nigeria. The aim of the project is to equip youths with advanced creative skills in animation & post-production through a comprehensive curriculum, market readiness training, and mentorship programs.

The Youth in Animation and Post-Production Initiative (YAPPI) stands as a transformative force in Nigeria’s creative economy, empowering 60,000 youth 80% female and 5% persons with disabilities (PWD) with advanced animation and post-production skills to drive employability and entrepreneurship.

At the midpoint of this 5-year initiative, the midline assessment is a critical Monitoring, Evaluation, Research, and Learning (MERL) endeavour to evaluate progress, validate the program’s Theory of Change (ToC), and inform strategic adaptations.

I anchored on the OECD-DAC evaluation criteria (Relevance, Coherence, Effectiveness, Efficiency, Impact, Sustainability), this assessment will deliver rigorous, evidence-based insights to enhance YAPPI’s impact.

By prioritizing inclusivity, localization, and Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP) principles, the assessment ensures that female youth and PWD are central to the evaluation process, fostering equitable outcomes in Nigeria’s creative industry.

Innovate Technologies to prevent and combat Sexual Violence
Understanding CRSV and GBV, FCT - Abuja, Nigeria

This training module was jointly developed by two organisations working on the frontlines of sexual violence response in Nigeria. We are NOT Weapons of War (WWoW) is a Paris-based NGO founded in 2014, dedicated to preventing and combating conflict-related sexual violence through holistic legal and survivor-centred solutions. Grassroots Researchers Association (GRA) is based in Northern Nigeria and advances social justice through participatory research, community empowerment, and direct support to survivors of sexual violence in IDP camps across Borno State.

I participated in the capacity building session by Biblotheques San Frontieres (BSF), who provided the technology using the Ideas Cube Box.

Gender-Based Violence (GBV) refers to violence directed at a person because of their gender. It takes many forms — physical, sexual, psychological, and economic — and can affect anyone: women, men, and children, in peacetime or conflict, at home or in institutions. Also, Conflict-Related Sexual Violence (CRSV) encompasses acts of sexual violence including rape, sexual slavery, forced prostitution, and forced pregnancy that are directly or indirectly linked to armed conflict. CRSV is not incidental; it is systematic and strategic, used as a weapon to terrorise, dominate, and displace populations. It is recognised as a war crime and crime against humanity under the Rome Statute and Geneva Conventions.

The impacts of CRSV are deep and multi-layered, affecting survivors physically (injuries, STIs, obstetric complications), psychologically (PTSD, depression, suicidal thoughts), socially (stigma, family rejection, forced silence), and economically (loss of income, increased dependency). Conflict and humanitarian crises amplify the risk of CRSV through the collapse of rule of law, displacement, presence of armed actors, gender inequality, breakdown of services, and widespread stigmatisation that silences survivors and shields perpetrators. Stigma remains one of the most harmful consequences of CRSV. Reducing it requires survivor-centred approaches, community engagement, accessible and discreet services, peer support networks, and accountability mechanisms that shift blame from survivors to perpetrators.

All GBV and CRSV response must be grounded in five core principles drawn from the IASC GBV Guidelines: survivor-centred care, confidentiality, informed consent, do no harm, and coordination across health, legal, psychosocial, and protection actors.

From Principles to Practice All actors were equipped with practical tools including case management frameworks, secure file systems, referral directories, and coordination mechanisms, ensuring survivors receive safe, dignified, and coordinated support from first contact through to case closure

August 2025

Emergency Aid Relief to Mokwa Flood Victims
Mokwa, Niger State, Nigeria

On 29 May 2025, the collapse of a nearby dam following intense rainfall triggered devastating flash floods in Mokwa LGA, Niger State. The disaster claimed over 150 lives, displaced more than 3,000 people, and caused extensive destruction of homes, farmlands, and critical community infrastructure. Displaced households—predominantly women, children, and lactating mothers—are currently living in overcrowded temporary shelters with severely limited access to food, safe drinking water, sanitation facilities, and other essential services.

Humanitarian conditions in the affected communities continued to deteriorate. Households faced acute food insecurity and lack access to safe water and adequate hygiene, heightening the risk of cholera and other waterborne disease outbreaks. Children under 12, who constitute more than half of the displaced population, are especially vulnerable to malnutrition, diarrhoeal diseases, and skin infections. The disaster also imposed significant psychosocial distress on survivors, while overcrowding and inadequate protection mechanisms have increased exposure to gender-based violence (GBV).

Despite initial efforts by government agencies and humanitarian partners, substantial gaps persist in the provision of food assistance, WASH supplies, and mental health and psychosocial support (MHPSS). Without timely, coordinated intervention, the affected population—particularly children—faces worsening health, nutrition, and protection outcomes.

In response, JRS Nigeria, with support from Irish Jesuit International (IJI) through Misean Cara, conducted distributions of food items, non-food items, and hygiene kits to displaced households in Mokwa in August 2025.

Leading the emergency aid relief, I ensured profiling of beneficiaries by JRS Emergency Response Team using appropriately designed tool/database with defined criteria. All selection processes adhered to humanitarian principles of impartiality and non-discrimination, with clear accountability and complaints handling mechanisms to ensure fairness. Priority was given to those who have not received comparable support from other agencies, to prevent duplication and promote equitable distribution of resources.

These criteria included:
  • Displaced households who lost homes, food stocks, and essential belongings due to the floods
  • Children under 12 years old, particularly those experiencing psychosocial distress, many of whom were separated from caregivers during evacuation or exposed to traumatic experiences.
  • Lactating mothers, pregnant women, and adolescent girls, facing heightened risks of malnutrition, poor hygiene, and gender-based violence (GBV) in displacement settings.
  • Elderly persons and persons with disabilities, who often face mobility and access barriers in emergency contexts.
  • Female-headed households, who are particularly vulnerable to exclusion from aid and exploitation in overcrowded shelter settings.
  • Child-Headed households resulting from the loss of parents (separated or unaccompanied), including those resulting from the outcome of the flood.
