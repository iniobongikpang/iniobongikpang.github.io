Safety Audit and Community Risk Mapping - USAID/IOM RRF Project
Damasak, Mobbar LGA, Borno State, Nigeria

As part of the Provision of Emergency WASH and Protection Services to Crisis-Affected Populations project, I oversaw a safety audit and community risk mapping exercise in Damasak, Mobbar LGA — targeting 22,534 vulnerable displaced persons and host community members in Northeast Nigeria.

Following a four-day capacity-building session to train enumerators on the safety audit and community mapping tool, a team of nine enumerators was deployed to conduct Focus Group Discussions (FGDs) with carefully selected community groups — including elderly men and women, youth, adolescents, and persons with disabilities — drawn from both the host community and the IDP camp. The process was designed to create a safe, non-judgmental space for participants to share their experiences and safety concerns freely.

Key Findings: The community risk mapping successfully identified specific locations posing significant danger to women and girls at night, both within the host community (Roundabout na Shagon Mumsey, Bakin Kasuwa, and Bakin Ruwa) and within the IDP camp (the camp entrance and Zone G). The exercise also surfaced the heightened vulnerability of persons with disabilities, particularly women and girls with mobility impairments who faced greater exposure to abuse, violence, and difficulty accessing essential services such as water points.

Community-Led Solutions: Mitigative measures were developed collaboratively with participants during the FGDs, including improved lighting in high-risk areas, encouraging women and girls to move in groups, intensifying community patrols, and pursuing legal accountability for offenders. The process also empowered participants, many of whom had never had their safety concerns formally. They acknowledged fostering a strong sense of ownership, solidarity, and community resilience.

Programmatic Outcomes: The findings directly informed JRS's advocacy efforts with key stakeholders, enabling evidence-based engagement on protection gaps. Identified issues, including the absence of a functional court in Damasak and the need for mobility aids and Individual Protection Assistants for persons with disabilities were escalated to the Protection Sector for follow-up action.

Lessons Learned: The exercise reinforced the critical importance of inclusive, participatory approaches in protection programming — ensuring that the voices of the most marginalised, particularly women, girls, and persons with disabilities, are actively heard, validated, and integrated into community safety planning.

November 2023 - April 2024

Educational Support for School-Aged IDP Children
IDP Camp, New Kuchingoro, Abuja - FCT, Nigeria

JRS Nigeria implemented a six-month project to improve educational access and WASH conditions for displaced children at the IDP Sharing Prosperity School, New Kuchingoro IDP Camp, FCT — Abuja. The camp hosts approximately 1,260 residents, predominantly from Borno and Adamawa States, many of whom have lived in displacement since 2014 with limited access to humanitarian support. I led the Country Program Team to facilitate project activities within the project cycle.

Stakeholder Engagement & Government Collaboration: Prior to implementation, I led introductory engagements with the IDP and host community stakeholders, and held a formal meeting with the FCT's Department of Special Needs Education (DSNE) — which oversees education for displaced persons in Abuja — to align the intervention with government priorities and ensure ownership, monitoring, and sustainability.

Education Outcomes: A capitation grant of ₦900,000 was provided to the school to cover tuition levies for 300 enrolled pupils and support teacher stipends, reducing financial barriers to school access. Learning materials — including exercise books, pens, pencils, erasers, and rulers — were distributed to all 300 pupils (156 female, 144 male), alongside a comprehensive set of teaching kits covering computer studies, agricultural science, security education, and early childhood learning charts.

Rehabilitation of Classrooms & Construction of Temporary Learning Space: As part of the educational support project at the IDP Sharing Prosperity School, I oversaw the rehabilitation of a dilapidated block of two classrooms that had remained uncompleted and posed safety risks to pupils and teachers. The classrooms were renovated to the required standard within the project cycle, directly addressing the school's critical shortage of functional learning spaces relative to its enrolled population. In addition, a temporary learning space was constructed to provide a safe and conducive environment specifically for Early Childhood Care Development (ECCD) learners, ensuring that the youngest and most vulnerable pupils had an appropriate space tailored to their developmental needs. Together, these infrastructure interventions improved the overall quality of the learning environment, supported pupil retention, and created safer conditions for both learners and teaching staff.

Teacher & SBMC Capacity Building: A structured training programme was delivered for teachers and School-Based Management Committee (SBMC) members, covering inclusive education and child protection, gender awareness and GBV prevention, mental health and psychosocial support (MHPSS), innovative teaching methodologies, and evidence-based decision-making. The SBMC was further equipped with skills in community consultation, resource mobilisation, and school governance.

WASH Outcomes: A gender-segregated toilet block was constructed, dramatically reducing the previously critical pupil-to-toilet ratio of 137:1. A handwashing station was also installed to promote hygiene practices and reduce the spread of infectious diseases. Hygiene kits — containing soap, toothbrush, toothpaste, detergent, Vaseline, and toilet tissue — were distributed to all 300 pupils, restoring dignity and supporting healthy habits for children living in displacement.

Handover & Recognition: All constructed and renovated facilities were formally handed over to the school in the presence of the DSNE Director, community leaders, and IDP stakeholders. The school headmaster expressed deep appreciation for JRS's swift and needs-driven response, and the DSNE Director commended the quality of the work and encouraged continued programming across other FCT locations.

MEAL Community of Practise - Moments, and Memories (JRS Rome, Nigeria, Kenya, and Uganda)!
Kampala, Uganda - East Africa.

JRS international launched a new Project Monitoring and Evaluation (PME) database, designed to integrate project design, performance monitoring, grant management, and financial tracking. JRS Nigeria served as one of the 3 pilot countries (West Africa Region, East Africa Region, and the Middle East) to use the database and provide feedback, loops, gaps in the use of the database.

This piloting was critical to ensuring that the system is fit for purpose before full-scale deployment. It allowed JRS Nigeria to test the system functionality, data architecture, and interoperability across programme, MEAL, grants, and finance components under real operational conditions. It further helped to identify gaps in indicator alignment, data workflows, user access controls, and reporting logic, while strengthening data quality assurance and validation processes.

After a year of piloting in my role as the Country MEAL Specialist in Nigeria being one of the pilot countries for the deployment and as part of the MEAL Community of Practice for JRS International, I participated in a technical learning and systems-strengthening session focused on operationalizing the platform across the project cycle. The session emphasized advanced monitoring data analysis, data validation, and system use, indicator harmonization, data quality assurance protocols, and system interoperability across programme, grants, and finance functions. In addition, 2023 programme data were submitted, reviewed, and validated to support the PME pilot phase.

The Community of Practice approach facilitated peer learning, shared problem-solving, and real-time feedback, contributing to iterative system improvement and informed decision-making ahead of full organizational rollout.

Through the piloting and CoP, we supported evidence-based refinement of indicators, reporting templates, and dashboards, ensuring alignment with results frameworks and donor requirements. Within Nigeria operations, I also enabled user capacity assessment, targeted training, and change management, reducing implementation risks and improving user adoption. Ultimately, this enhanced an overall system reliability, data credibility, and decision-making effectiveness, laying a strong foundation for scalable, accountable, and learning-driven programme management.

The moments and memories lie within JRS’s accompaniment of the refugees (South Sudan, Democratic Republic of Congo, Sudan, Somalia, Burundi, Rwanda, and Ethiopia) they serve in Kampala and Adjumani, tourist sites including the National Mosque, and the Lake Victoria.

Climate-Smart Agriculture Project Implementation Validation - Brown Field Day
Project Communities across Adamawa and Borno States, NorthEast Nigeria

A Brown Field Day is an agricultural extension event, primarily held in Nigeria and West Africa, that brings farmers, researchers, and stakeholders together to showcase, demonstrate, and adopt improved, high-yield crop varieties (such as wheat, sorghum, and maize) and sustainable farming practices (e.g., mechanization, reduced burning).

As the Country MEAL Specialist, I visited the 5 project locations across Adamawa (Girei, Michika, and Mubi South LGAs) and Borno (Hawul and Biu LGAs) States to provide a real-time analysis during the Brown field day. The term 'Brown Field Day' was derived from mature, harvest-ready crops, such as beans, maize, and groundnut characterized by their brown color indicating ripeness. The focus of the demonstration centered on these staple crops in Northeast Nigeria, aiming to guide farmers on optimal cultivation practices to sustain harvests amidst challenges posed by population growth and climate change.

Additionally, the initiative sought to establish a unified market for agricultural inputs in Northeast Nigeria, addressing obstacles in the supply and marketing of crop protection products, fertilizers, and herbicides.

While giving the details of the practical experiment, one of the beneficiary farmers said, The demonstration has vividly shown farmers how their yield can be when they apply fertilizer or when they use organic manure, hence giving them a practical result to adopt. She further said, This has proved to them that the use of an improved variety of seeds would make the yield more plenty and resourceful.

Key Features of the Brown Field Day were:

  • Technology Demonstration: The events often focus on showcasing new crop varieties like the Sammaz 52 maize, demonstrating their resilience to farmers in the field
  • Capacity Building: The days act as a platform for farmer training, specifically focusing on boosting food security and adopting climate-smart agriculture. The Local Government Extension agents participated in the event at their respective LGAs.
  • Focus on Sustainability: The introduction of carbon credits for farmers and the promotion of sustainable, non-destructive farming techniques.

Overall, these field days serve as a critical bridge between agricultural research and on-farm application, improving both food security and local livelihoods.

Multi-Sectoral Needs Assessment (MSNA) for Displaced Persons
New Kuchingoro IDP Camp, Games Village. FCT - Abuja, Nigeria

JRS Nigeria conducted a Multi-Sectoral Needs Assessment (MSNA) at the New Kuchingoro IDP Camp, Games Village, FCT — Abuja, home to approximately 1,260 residents, the majority of whom are displaced indigenes of Borno and Adamawa States who have lived in the camp since February 2014. I led the assessment, aimed to identify gaps in access to services across Education, Health, WASH, Protection, and Livelihoods, to inform prioritised and focused humanitarian response planning.

Methodology: A qualitative approach was employed, using a Key Informant Interview (KII) with the Camp Secretary, supplemented by a physical tour of the camp to observe conditions across all sectors.

Key Findings were:

Education: An ECCD and primary school established in 2018 serves 249–275 pupils from both the IDP community and host community, staffed largely by 11 volunteer teachers receiving inconsistent allowances. While basic learning materials are provided each term, classrooms, instructional resources, and teacher remuneration remain critically insufficient. A School-Based Management Committee (SBMC) and PTA provide governance and accountability oversight.

Health: A mini health centre established by the German Embassy in 2015 offers free primary health care, though services are constrained by a volunteer nurse who visits only once weekly due to high transportation costs. Common health concerns include malaria, typhoid, diarrhoea, and skin diseases. Access to secondary health facilities is limited by distance and cost.

WASH: Water access relies on a manual hand pump borehole and a nearby stream, with no water purification practices in place. A second borehole provided by NMRC is currently non-functional, pending solar conversion. Toilet facilities — located approximately 500 metres from living shelters with no nearby water points — have encouraged open defecation, posing significant public health risks

Food Security & Livelihoods: Residents rely on farm work, manual labour, and small-scale trade for income, with livelihoods further strained by Nigeria's subsidy removal and rising cost of living. A skills acquisition centre within the camp has trained residents in tailoring, cosmetology, and confectionery, though graduates face barriers to market entry including taxation and limited patronage.

Protection: The camp's internal leadership structure manages disputes effectively, with police and military engaged for serious cases. No incidents of sexual violence or harassment within the camp were reported during the assessment period.

Priority Needs & Recommendations: Residents identified education as their foremost priority need. Key recommendations include provisioning of learning materials and increased teacher support, regular engagement of medical personnel and medication supply, solar-powered borehole installation with water reticulation throughout the camp, and business empowerment support for skills-trained residents to establish livelihoods outside the camp.

June - August 2022

Stakeholder Engagement - Understanding the Needs of the Displaced Population
Reconciliation and Livelihood Assessment within communities in Adamawa and Borno States, NorthEast Nigeria

The violent conflict in Nigeria’s Northeast has killed hundreds of thousands of persons, displacing millions of people, devastating thousands of communities, and slowing down the economy. This displacement led to the establishment of several IDP camps in Borno, Adamawa and Yobe States by the different state governments and the subsequent arrival of humanitarian organizations to provide much needed relief services to the displaced persons. Following the announcement by the Borno State Government on plans to close all IDP camps in the state and return all displaced persons to their homes, and the concerns raised among Nigerians and the humanitarian sector, most of the communities were not considered safe as the crisis is still ongoing.

Due to secondary displacement, it is anticipated that the presence of IDPs in these very remote villages with already overstretched resources and infrastructure might lead to increased competition for survival and eventually conflict if the situation is not well managed. The livelihood and reconciliation assessment in Numan and Demsa LGAs within Adamawa State and in Biu LGA, Borno State were to guide prioritised humanitarian interventions.

Majority of the households were engaged in farming (248, 72.1%) while other had at least one form of livelihood including running personal (self-employed) or family business (54, 15.7%), or doing housework (11, 3.2%). As part of the weekly coping strategy for lack of food, 82.8% of the household had to rely on less preferred or cheaper food at least once or more days every week, 71.5% had to borrow/rely on food from friends or relatives at least once or more days every week, 74% had to limit their portion size at meal times at least once or more days every week, 67% had to restrict consumption by adults in order for small children to eat either once or more days in a week and 72.7% had to reduce the number of meals eaten more than once in a week.

Livelihood opportunities can also be enhanced through leveraging on locally available resources within the various communities. During the interactions, the respondents agreed that locally available resources were:

  • Lands that are accessible for farming activities and grazing livestock
  • Markets that are accessible for trading activities

Overall, the preferred livelihoods in order of priority were farming, livestock rearing and trading. Others mentioned were tailoring, carpentry and hair dressing.

Using methodologies like the Conflict Tree Analysis, and the Dividers and Connectors, I deep dived to fully understand the conflict within the community/LGA.

Dividers are those things or factors that increase tensions between people or groups. Dividers are those things we want to stop. Within the communities of assessment, several dividers were identified including:

  • During religious gatherings, preaching becomes a divider because most of the leaders or scholars have as a lack of understanding or misinterpretation ended up in misleading people (their followers) with wrong teachings.
  • In our community, as a result of the crises and displacement, most of the persons have become unemployed due to the fact that they have lost their sources of livelihood. Therefore, access to land for farming activities has become a divider as it is limited.
  • During our school tournaments, officiating matches or games becomes a divider because it is felt that it is bias and the officiating personnel/referee is unfair especially towards certain ethnic groups.
  • Within the region and country, there has been a pending strike action initiated by the Academic Staff Union of Universities (ASUU) for tertiary education institutions.
  • In Biu LGA, during routine elections of persons from political parties to public offices, rigging becomes a divider as certain persons are paid to ensure the system does not run freely and fairly.
  • In Shafaron community – Numan LGA, access to potable water is a divider as most times not everyone has equal access to the water source and all times.
  • Distribution or sharing of assets (family inheritance) has been a divider as most of the family members have always neglected their siblings (especially the females) during the sharing of their parent’s inheritance.

Connectors are those things or factors that reduce tensions between people or groups and lead to and promote constructive collaboration. Connectors are things we want to support. Through discussions, the team identified a few connectors within the communities including:

  • Organizing sports, tournaments, competitions and games within our communities have been a great connector.
  • Inter-school competitions including sports, debate, quiz as well as PTA meetings have been a great connector between youths and adults.
  • Organizing the “Kwete festival”, this is an annual inter-community wrestling festival in Numan LGA which allows people compete for a title and the “Vunon festival” an annual exhibition event in Demsa LGA which brings people from various kingdoms to buy and sell specific locally produced items. These events serve as connectors to the people, communities and states as various traditional leaders during these come together to discuss the challenges and progress needs of their communities.
  • Activities including celebrations of marriages, birthdays, religious activities have become connectors as people have through these gatherings ensure conflicts is addressed.
  • Forming social groups like community associations, age groups as well as savings/contribution groups (adashe) who meet regularly have served as connectors.

Overall, watching of football matches in public viewing centers was agreed to be both dividers and connectors
