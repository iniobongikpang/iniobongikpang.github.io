Training on Accountability and Setting up Community Feedback and Response Mechanisms (FRM) Systems
Takum, Ussa, Kurmi and Sardauna LGAs, Taraba State.

In July 2022, I facilitated a training for project staff on accountability and setting up Community Feedback and Response Mechnaisms for our project implemenation sites in Taraba State to strengthen their understanding of responsible and transparent project implementation. The training emphasized the importance of balancing and responding to the needs of all stakeholders including beneficiaries, donors, partners, and the organization while delivering quality humanitarian and development interventions.

Participants explored key principles of accountability, including transparency, participation, complaint and response mechanisms, and evaluation. The training also highlighted the importance of integrating learning into project implementation by encouraging reflection, using monitoring data for decision-making, and adapting programs to better meet community needs.

A major focus was placed on strengthening feedback and complaint mechanisms to ensure communities can safely share concerns, suggestions, or complaints about project activities. Staff were introduced to systems such as hotlines, community forums, and digital reporting tools that support timely responses and improved accountability to affected populations.

Overall, the training enhanced staff capacity to promote transparency, protect vulnerable populations, and ensure that humanitarian interventions remain responsive, inclusive, and accountable to the communities they serve.

June 2022

Impact Assessment - Bureau of Population, Refugees, and Migration (BPRM) Project
Protection and Health Services for Cameroonian Refugees in Taraba State

The Jesuit Refugee Service (JRS), with funding from the Bureau of Population, Refugees, and Migration (BPRM), implemented a Protection and Health Services project for Cameroonian refugees in Taraba State, Nigeria. The project serves refugees fleeing the ongoing Anglophone Crisis in Cameroon's North-West and South-West regions, targeting four Local Government Areas: Ussa, Takum, Kurmi, and Sardauna.

As the Country MEAL SPecialist, I led the team to carry out an impact assessment which was conducted between September 2021 and May 2022, surveying 565 households across all four LGAs using a mixed-methods approach — combining household surveys, focus group discussions, and key informant interviews.

Key Findings during the assessment were:

Health and WASH JRS-supported primary healthcare facilities were rated highly, with 86.54% of beneficiaries satisfied with service quality. Distribution of insecticide-treated nets contributed to a reduction in malaria cases, with 78.36% of respondents using them regularly. However, access to potable water remains a critical challenge — many communities rely on open streams vulnerable to contamination, putting residents at risk of cholera, diarrhea, and typhoid fever. About 33% of households do not treat drinking water before consumption. Sanitation Over 66% of households use unhygienic pit latrines, and nearly 60% share toilet facilities with other households. About 30% of children under five practice open defecation. These conditions significantly elevate the risk of disease outbreaks.

Protection & Safety Sexual and Gender-Based Violence (SGBV) remains a serious concern, particularly for adolescent girls. Key enablers include inadequate security lighting, shared accommodations, and limited access to safe toilets. JRS has conducted sensitization campaigns in schools, churches, and community meetings, and provides medical, psychosocial, and legal support to survivors. Social Cohesion Relations between refugees and host community members are generally peaceful — 86.53% of respondents reported no unresolved conflicts. However, tensions arise around the distribution of humanitarian aid and competition for limited resources such as shelter and farmland. Established conflict resolution mechanisms exist and are widely known at both community (94%) and LGA (96%) levels.

Key Recommendations

  • Upgrade PHC facilities and train local traditional healers in first aid to bridge healthcare access gaps
  • Introduce sustainable livelihood programmes alongside NFI distributions to reduce poverty dependency
  • Sensitize host communities ahead of distributions to reduce tensions
  • Provide safe, accessible toilets and reliable potable water sources near refugee settlements
  • Include more vulnerable host community members in relief distributions
  • Expand vocational training opportunities in tailoring, carpentry, agriculture, and other trades

This assessment was conducted by JRS in partnership with UNHCR, the Nigerian Red Cross Society, and local emergency management agencies. It reflects the situation of over 17,600 Cameroonian refugees residing in Taraba State as of the assessment period.

Moments and Memories - Nairobi, Kenya - East Africa!

JRS International, following the need to continually strengthen her systems and ensure seamless Beneficiary Registration, Programme, Monitoring and Evaluation, Human Resources Management, and Grant Management designed a holistic Information Management System.

It will strengthen data governance through defined roles, access controls, validation protocols, and audit trails, while enabling real-time analysis and evidence-based decision-making. Importantly, this robust IMS supports organizational learning, adaptive management, and compliance with donor and regulatory requirements, ultimately improving programme effectiveness, transparency, and impact.

January 2022

Stakeholder Engagement on Protection and Education Needs of Cameroonian Refugees in Taraba State
Kurmi, Sardauna, Takum, and Ussa LGAs

In 2021, the Jesuit Refugee Service (JRS), with funding from United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR), implemented the Protection for Cameroonian Refugees Project in Taraba State. The project delivered education and protection services to Persons of Concern (PoCs) residing within host communities across Takum, Ussa, Kurmi, and Sardauna Local Government Areas (LGAs).

During implementation, I led the team to conduct a targeted profiling and needs assessment focusing on PoCs under 18 years of age. This initiative was informed by observations that while household-level interventions were being provided, the specific needs of children and adolescents were often overlooked.

The assessment covered:
  • Out-of-school children
  • Children with special needs
  • Unaccompanied and Separated Children (UASCs), including foster parents profiled under livelihood assessments
  • Young persons under 25 years previously enrolled in higher institutions

Findings revealed critical service gaps and highlighted child-specific vulnerabilities that differed from broader household needs.

To facilitate coordinated response and action planning, JRS convened a stakeholder forum on 26 January 2022 at the Pastoral Centre, Jalingo. The assessment findings were shared with key Government Ministries, Departments, and Agencies (MDAs), including:

  • Ministries of Education, Justice, Women Affairs, Health, Humanitarian Affairs and Social Development
  • Office of the Secretary to the State Government
  • Taraba State Universal Basic Education Board (SUBEB)
  • Local Government Education Authorities (LGEAs)
  • Nigeria Immigration Service (NIS)
  • National Commission for Refugees, Migrants and Internally Displaced Persons (NCFRMI)
  • Taraba State Emergency Management Agency (TSEMA)
  • Nigerian Red Cross Society (NRCS)
  • UNHCR

These institutions were identified as critical actors in strengthening protection, education, and welfare services for refugees in Taraba State.

Stakeholders highlighted several emerging concerns:

  • Security risks associated with the non-camp (community-based) refugee settlement approach in Taraba State, compared to camp-based systems in other Nigerian states and countries.
  • Documentation challenges, including lack of access to voter registration, national identity documentation, and birth registration for newborns—raising risks of statelessness.
  • Welfare and protection of refugee rights, including inquiries about pathways for asylum in third countries.
  • The need for structured support (including stipends) for refugee leaders to effectively coordinate community-level leadership and management.
  • Limited livelihood opportunities driving cross-border movements in search of food and income.
  • Shortage of essential drugs in primary health facilities.
  • Increased protection risks, including heightened vulnerability to sexually transmitted diseases (STDs) linked to survival strategies.

The UNHCR Head of Field Office (HOFO) in Taraba addressed concerns regarding “integration.” He clarified that refugees in Taraba are not formally integrated but are living within host communities under a community-based settlement model. He explained that camp-based approaches are increasingly discouraged because infrastructure developed in camps often becomes redundant once refugees voluntarily repatriate.

He emphasized that investments made such as support to public schools, health facilities, and provision of potable water remain sustainable assets within host communities, aligning with broader development objectives in Taraba State. The JRS Health Supervisor further outlined ongoing health interventions benefiting both refugees and host community members, reinforcing a rights-based and inclusive service delivery approach.

The meeting concluded with jointly identified action points aimed at strengthening coordination among stakeholders. Both the JRS Area Team Lead and the UNHCR Head of Field Office encouraged sustained collaboration to ensure that interventions maximize positive impact for both refugee and host communities during the implementation year.

December 2021

Cash and Voucher Assistance (CVA) for Cameroonian Refugees in Taraba State
CBI for Food across Kurmi, Sardauna, Takum, and Ussa LGAs

In response to the influx of Cameroonian refugees into Taraba State in February and March 2021, I led the Cash-Based Intervention (CBI) for food to support newly arrived families fleeing violence in Cameroon’s North West and South West regions. The crisis, driven by conflict between government forces and armed non-state actors, forced many families to cross into Nigeria, particularly into communities within Sardauna LGA.

To address urgent food needs, cash assistance was provided to registered Persons of Concern (PoCs) across Takum, Ussa, Kurmi, and Sardauna LGAs. Each beneficiary received ₦13,800 over three months (₦4,600 per month) to enable them purchase food items based on their household priorities.

Key Achievements:
  • Reached 1,622 households (1,020 refugee and 602 host community households)
  • Supported 3,194 individuals (2,592 refugees and 602 host community members).
  • Implemented distribution across 9 strategic locations, expanding from the initially planned 7 points to improve access.

Distribution points included Takum Town (Takum LGA), Fikyu (Ussa LGA), Baissa and Abong (Kurmi LGA), and Gembu, Nguroje, Yerimaru, Ndumnyaji, Warkaka, and Sahkaka (Sardauna LGA).

This was intended to ensure reduced challenges faced by beneficiaries (financial and logistical barriers) in accessing distribution centers, particularly in hard-to-reach areas of Sardauna LGA, where some had to travel for over five hours and crossed rivers. In Takum LGA, some beneficiaries had to travel over an hour to reach distribution points. Additionally, relocation of beneficiaries after registration required flexible scheduling to ensure no eligible household was excluded.

In addition to handling a pay point, i provided oversight functions to other payment points across the distribution centers. In addition, I coordinated the daily reconciliation from all the established payment points at each distribution center, ensuring quality assurance, and data integrity.

The intervention successfully improved access to food, reduced immediate vulnerability, and supported both refugee and host communities during that critical period of displacement.

June 2021

Maiden Project Performance Review Meeting
Gembu - Sardauna LGAs, Taraba State

Performance review meetings are essential for ensuring that a project remains aligned with its objectives and delivers the intended results. They provide an opportunity to assess progress against planned targets, compare actual achievements with expected outputs, and identify any gaps early. By regularly reviewing performance data, teams can ensure that activities stay on track and that resources are being used effectively and efficiently.

It also strengthens accountability and transparency within the project team and among partners. Team members report on their responsibilities, discuss challenges, and agree on corrective actions where needed. This process improves coordination, supports evidence-based decision-making, and reduces the risk of delays or mismanagement. It also prepares the project for donor reporting, audits, and evaluations by ensuring that documentation and performance indicators are consistently monitored.

As the M&E Specialist, I initiatied the maiden Project Performance Review Meeting for the Organization, and it was organized to have a holistic overview of the project implementation in line with the workplan and defined indicators. This meeting took place in Gembu between 23rd and 24th June 2021, with all project staff involved. This provided an overall picture of the progress and achievements while also identifying gaps before the end of the first phase (June 30). Re-strategized programmatic approaches have been employed to address identified gaps and ameliorative measures taken to ensure that expected targets are achieved timely. Developed action plans are to guide units for effective implementation towards project timelines in the second half of the year.

Furthermore, the performance review meeting promoted learning and adaptation. It created space to reflect on what worked well and what needed improvement, encouraging adaptive management and continuous improvement. By recognizing achievements and addressing performance gaps constructively, the meeting also enhanced staff motivation and contributed to overall project success and sustainability.

Training on PSEA, Child Protection, GBV and Remote Data Collection using ODK
Takum, Ussa, Kurmi & Sardauna LGAs, Taraba State - Nigeria

Training and re-training are processes that support system strengthening for effective programme implementation. In a bid to improve the capacity of JRS monitors across project locations (24 males 17 females), I facilitated sessions of trainings (Theoretical and Practical) across programme implementation areas.

These trainings included sessions on Prevention of Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA), Child Protection (CP), Gender Based Violence (GBV) and remote data collection for improved border and detention centre monitoring. Key areas of intensive discussion during the training included:

  • Border monitoring and protection concern (theoretical and practical).
  • Understanding the concept of child protection (theoretical and practical).
  • Protection of Sexual and Exploitation Abuse (PSEA).
  • Introduction of reviewed tools for Border and Detention Monitoring using ODK for remote data collection.

June 2021

Migrating Paper-Based Data Collection Tools to ODK
Takum, Ussa, Kurmi & Sardauna LGAs, Taraba State - Nigeria

Driven by the need for improved efficiency, accuracy, data security and the post-COVID 19 realities of virtual learnings, I initiated and migrated from paper-based data collection to ODK (Open Data Kit) for border and detention monitoring within the Cameroonian Refugee Operations. The paper-based systems were time-consuming, prone to human error, and often resulted in delays in data entry, cleaning, and analysis. As protection actors, where timely identification of rights violations, detention conditions, and urgent case referrals is critical; such delays limited the ability to respond quickly and effectively.

Transitioning to ODK enabled real-time or near real-time data submission from the field, reducing reporting lag and strengthened evidence-based advocacy and response. Built-in validation checks and constraints minimized data entry errors, improved consistency, and enhanced overall data quality. The digital system also allowed for better tracking of cases, easier aggregation of trends across border points and detention facilities, and faster generation of reports for coordination meetings and donor compliance.

Importantly, the shift improved data protection and confidentiality, which are central to refugee protection programming. Sensitive personal information could be encrypted, password-protected, and securely stored, reducing the risk of data loss or unauthorized access that is common with paper forms. Overall, migrating to ODK strengthened monitoring, enhanced accountability, improved response time to protection concerns, and aligned data management practices with humanitarian standards for safe and ethical information handling.

Within the forst quarter of resumption, I carreid out a review of all existing Data Collection Tools (DCTs) in collaboration with various program units and migrated all the paper-based DCTs to remote data collection tools. Further, I facilitated sessions of training and re-training for staff and community-based Protection and Border Monitors to build their capacity on mobile data collection methodologies (Theoretical and Practical) reaching 23 staff (16 males and 7 females) and 41 (24 males 17 females) monitors.
